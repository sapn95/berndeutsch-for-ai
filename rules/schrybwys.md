# Bärndütschi Schrybwys — the rulebook

Write Bernese German in the codified **schriftsprach-nah** system: the one
oriented on the High-German Schriftbild, established by Werner Marti and Ruth
Bietenhard. The other established system, Eugen Dieth's lautgetreu spelling, is
equally valid and is not what this file describes.

A dialect has no binding orthography, so nothing here is an authority. It is
still perfectly possible to write Bernese badly, and one rule outranks the rest:

> **Be consistent within a text.** Either system is fine. Mixing them is what
> reads as wrong.

Provenance and licences are recorded in `NOTICE`. Verify word choice with
`bdw <wort>` rather than inventing a form.

---

## What gives a text away

Five things mark a text as not-quite-Bernese, in roughly the order a Bernese
reader notices them.

1. **`nöd`.** The negation is `nid` or `nit`. `nöd` is Zurich, and one of them
   is enough to break the illusion.
2. **Ä-inflation.** An unstressed `e` is an `e`: `Gipfeli`, `Meiteli`,
   `Bibeli`, `Härzlechi Grüess`. Nobody in Bern pronounces `Gipfäli`.
3. **`ds` where `z` belongs.** `ds` is the article «das», `z` is the
   preposition «zu». `ds Modul` but `z Bärn`, `z tüe`.
4. **`iä`, `uä`, `üä`.** The Zwielaut is written `ie`, `ue`, `üe`: `Bier`,
   `gnue`, `wüescht`, `Bueb`, `müed`.
5. **No l-vocalisation.** `viu`, `Gäud`, `schnäu`, `Chüngu`, `Löu`. Writing
   `viel`, `Geld`, `schnell` reads as High German with dialect words in it.

## Vowels

**`y` versus `i` versus `ii`.** `y` is the closed vowel, short or long, and is
never doubled. `i` is the open one. This is a real four-way distinction in the
spoken language, not a spelling nicety: Bernese contrasts `Rytter` on a horse
with `Ritter` in armour, and `Bruuch` (a custom) with `Bruch` (a break).

| sound | written | examples |
|---|---|---|
| closed, long | `y` | `schrybe`, `gsy`, `Zyt`, `mys` |
| closed, short | `y` | `wyt`, `myni` |
| open, long | `ii` | `viil`, `Riis` |
| open, short | `i` | `Sitzig`, `Schritt`, `lige` |

`ie` is written only where both vowels are genuinely pronounced as a diphthong:
`verschiebe`, `Bier`, `Lied`. Where High German `ie` gives a short open `i` in
Bernese, the lengthening `e` drops out: `Fride`, `sibe`, `ligt`.

Loanwords are outside this rule: `d Kiwi`, `d Software`.

**The swallowed `e`.** The nearly inaudible `e` is still written. Neither
replacing it with `ä` nor dropping it belongs to this system: `vergässe`, not
`värgässe` or `vrgässe`; `Gufe`, not `Gufä`.

**Doubled vowels** are kept where High German has them (`Saal`, `Boot`) and not
introduced where it does not, except as a length mark: `Aabe`, `nüüt`.

**`eu` and `äu` become `öi`:** `Fröid`, `Höi`, `nöi`, `tröime`.

**The lengthening `h`** survives where the length does: `fahre`, `meh`, `gseh`,
and also where the vowel shifts: `näh`, `gah`, `verstah`. It is dropped where
High German's separating `h` turns into a Bernese `j`: `blüeje`, `Chräje`. It
is never invented where High German has none: `schyne`, `gä`, `la`.

**Separable prefixes** double their vowel when they stand alone: `mir fahre
uuse`, `mir fange aa`.

## Consonants

Ordered by what they are, not by spelling-guide convention: the two sound
changes that define the dialect first, then the joint they create, then the two
places where the spelling merely follows a convention.

**l-vocalisation** is the signature sound, and its boundary is where most
mistakes happen. Three cases:

1. A single `l` before a consonant or at the end of a word becomes `u`:
   `aut`, `Gäud`, `Chüngu`, `Löu`, `viu`, `schnäu`.
2. A double `ll` becomes `u` as well, even between vowels: `wölle` gives
   `wöue`, and `alli` gives `aui`.
3. A single `l` between two vowels stays an `l`: `hole`, `Zahle`, `male`.

The dictionary supplies the minimal pair itself: the verb is `hole` with the
`l` intact, but its participle is `ghout`, because there the `l` stands before
a `t`. Where `u + l` would produce a double `u`, keep the `l` rather than
doubling the vowel.

**Velarisation: `nd` becomes `ng`.** `Hang`, `Ching`, `mitenang`, `angers`.
Keeping the High-German `nd` throughout is also acceptable, as long as it is
kept throughout.

**The linking `n`** is what those vowel-final words need when the next word
also starts with a vowel. All three spellings are fine as long as one of them
is used throughout: `Wo ni das gseh ha`, `Won i das gseh ha`,
`Wo-n-i das gseh ha`. Next to an article it merges without a hyphen:
`är rennt wi ne Chüngu`, `mir hei ne Bäse bruucht`.

**`sp` and `st`** are the one place where the spelling deliberately ignores the
pronunciation. They are written as in High German at the start of a word, and
also after a prefix or a verb particle: `Stei`, `starch`, `verstecke`,
`spile`, `umespringe`. Inside and at the end of a word they follow the ear
instead: `luschtig`, `Wäschpi`, `Poscht`. Place names and family names keep
`Sp` and `St` in every system: `Spiez`, never `Schpiez`.

**Doubling** follows the ear again, and only where Bernese actually doubles:
`Büez`, `chouffe`, `bätte`. In the past participle the High-German `ge-` drops
before a strong consonant: `bruucht`, `trunke`, `kämpft`. Verbs whose stem
starts with a soft `b`, `d` or `g` before a vowel double that consonant
instead: `ggange`, `ddänkt`, `bbout`. Three things never double: `ch`
(`Loch`, `Sache`), and `tz`, `ck`, `dt` (`hetze`, `locker`, `Stäcke`). `ng`
does not double either, but may be reinforced with a second `g`: `Unggle`.

## Grammar

**There is no preterite.** Alemannic lost it, so the perfect does the work:
«wir gingen» is `mir sy gange`. With no preterite there is no pluperfect
either, and a double perfect fills the gap: `Wo ds Telefon glütet het, bini
scho gange gsy.`

**The subjunctive is used far more than in High German:** `Chäm öpper no eis
cho näh?` A periphrastic form with `täte` is common in modern usage.

**`ga`, `cho` and `la` double:** `I ga ga schaffe`, `I chume cho hälfe`, `I la
der Vatter (la) grüesse`, where the second `la` is optional.

**Two and three decline:**

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | `zwe Here` | `zwo Froue` | `zwöi Ching` |
| 3 | `drei Here` | `drei Froue` | `drü Ching` |

**Politeness is the second person plural**, not the third, as in French and as
in 18th-century German. Hence `Grüessech`. Written out, `dihr` is preferable to
`dir`, which collides with the dative «dir». After the verb the short form is
usual: `gseht 'er`, `wüsst 'er`.

**Apostrophes** are optional in a shortened «es»: `Wi hei mer's` or `Wi hei
mers`. The article `d` never takes one: `d Lüt`, `d Wäut`.

**Loanwords keep their original spelling.** This is a principle of the system,
not an oversight: `Handy`, `Router`, `Pandemie`.

## Bern, not its neighbours

Bernese is not generic Swiss German, and the most common way a text stops
sounding Bernese is a neighbouring form slipping in.

- negation is `nid` or `nit`, never `nöd`
- `gsy` for «gewesen»
- vocabulary that is Bernese and not pan-Swiss: `gäng`/`geng` (always), `äuä`
  (presumably), `Modi` (girl), `Gieu` (boy), `Hegu` (knife), `öppis`, `öpper`,
  `mängisch`, `chli`, `gäu`, `zwätschge`, `Chuchi`, `Rüebli`, `Bäse`

Variation inside the canton is real and fine. The Seeland and the Oberaargou
round the long /aː/ and say `wüu` and `Töu`; south of the city you hear `giit`
and `luuft` for `geit` and `louft`. Pick one and stay in it.
